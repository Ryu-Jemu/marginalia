decisions:
  - decision: Give each side its own researcher.
    why: >-
      Two agents arguing over one shared retrieval will converge on whatever
      that retrieval surfaced. Separate researchers mean the disagreement is
      about evidence rather than about phrasing.
    rejected:
      - A single retrieval step feeding both sides. Cheaper, and it collapses the debate into agreement.
  - decision: Let "cannot determine" be a real verdict.
    why: >-
      Absence of evidence is not evidence of falsity. A checker that must answer
      true or false will manufacture a lean on thin sourcing, which is the
      failure mode that matters most for this kind of tool.
    rejected:
      - Forcing a binary verdict with a confidence score attached. The score gets ignored and the label does not.
  - decision: Compute the final figure in Python.
    why: >-
      The agents produce arguments and citations. Turning those into a
      confidence grade is arithmetic, and a model doing arithmetic in prose will
      occasionally get it wrong in a way nothing catches.
    notes:
      - examiner-determinism
  - decision: Check citations in code as well as in the prompt.
    why: >-
      Telling a model not to invent a citation reduces the rate; it does not
      make it zero. Verifying that a quoted passage exists in the retrieved
      document is the half of that defence which cannot be talked out of.
  - decision: Bound the debate with explicit termination conditions.
    why: >-
      A judge that can send the researchers back for more evidence needs a
      reason to stop, or a hard claim will loop until the budget runs out.
    notDone:
      - Tuning those bounds against real usage. They were set by hand.
boundaries:
  - There is no evaluation set, so I cannot say how often its verdicts are right.
  - Deployed on a free instance that sleeps; the first request after idle is slow.
  - A working prototype, not a finished product.

The design question was not how to make a model check a claim. It was how to
stop a model from agreeing with itself.

A prosecutor and a defender each argue over three rounds, and each is fed by its
own researcher, so the two sides arrive with different evidence rather than
different adjectives applied to the same evidence. A judge weighs the exchange
and can send either researcher back for more, bounded by explicit termination
conditions so a hard claim cannot loop indefinitely.

Two decisions carry the rest of it. The first is that "cannot determine" is a
verdict the system is allowed to return: absence of evidence is not evidence of
falsity, and a checker forced to choose between true and false will invent a
lean on thin sourcing. The second is that the final confidence figure is
computed in Python rather than produced by the
model.<N id="examiner-determinism" /> The agents argue; they do not get to do
the arithmetic about their own argument. Citations are verified against the
retrieved documents in code, not only forbidden in the prompt, because a
prompt-level rule lowers the rate of invented quotes without ever reaching zero.

What I cannot tell you is whether it is right. There is no evaluation set, no
labelled corpus, and no measurement of verdict accuracy — so this is a working
prototype demonstrating a design, not a tool whose output I would ask anyone to
rely on.<N id="examiner-completeness" />
