import N from '../../components/note/N.astro';

Two slices, urgent and bulk, priced with a three-part tariff: a subscription
fee, an included allowance, and an overage rate. Set them too high and customers
leave; too low and the revenue is not there. The policy has to price against its
own effect on the customer base, which is what makes it a sequential problem
rather than an optimisation.

The result is that the learned policy beats a max-price baseline by a margin
that grows with how sensitive customers are to
price.<N id="rl-uplift" /> The number I would defend in an interview, though, is
not the largest one. It is the control: the same algorithm trained with its
discount factor set to zero — able to learn, unable to look ahead — is
indistinguishable from the baseline at every
setting.<N id="rl-myopic" /> That is the experiment that says the advantage
comes from anticipating churn rather than from having used reinforcement
learning.

Two things cut against the headline. Decomposing the revenue gap by slice shows
almost all of it in one of the two, so the aggregate figure describes one slice
wearing the average as a
disguise.<N id="rl-embb-attribution" /> And at the lowest churn sensitivity
the advantage is not significant at all — a result worth keeping, because it
marks the boundary of where the approach is worth anything.

The algorithm comparison went the same way: a deterministic method landed below,
another on-policy method was statistically indistinguishable, and reporting that
honestly is more useful than presenting the choice as
obvious.<N id="rl-algorithms" /> The paper took first author and a silver award
at the conference's paper competition.<N id="rl-award" />
